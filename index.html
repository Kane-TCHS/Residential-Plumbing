Follow the water

Roman aqueducts supplied public fountains and baths, and public latrines carried waste into sewers. Few homes had private connections. A modern home brings potable water to fixtures under pressure and sends used water out through a separate system that relies on gravity.

Pressure moves drinking water

Main, service, meter, shutoff
The public main feeds the home’s service line. The meter measures water use, and a shutoff valve stops the flow.
Fixtures and water heater
Fixtures include sinks, showers, and toilets. The heater takes in cold water and supplies the hot-water network.
Trunks and branches
Trunks carry water toward several fixtures. Branches take water from a trunk to individual fixtures or small fixture groups.

Gravity drains wastewater

Trap and vent
Water in the trap blocks sewer gases. Vent piping lets air enter and leave the drainage system to protect that water seal.
Drain, stack, and cleanout
Drain pipes carry wastewater downhill. A stack is a vertical drain or vent. A cleanout provides access to clear a blockage.
Sewer lateral and sanitary main
The lateral connects the house drain to the public sanitary sewer. Storm drains carry runoff separately.
Compare the supply and drain systems in the slide deck

Why drawings and codes matter. Different line patterns let a reader distinguish pipes even on a black-and-white plan. Plumbing codes protect drinking water and public health. Supply sizing depends on demand, pressure, length, and elevation. Drain and vent sizing depends in part on the load from connected fixtures. For this concept plan, use the classroom checks below.

Draw your plumbing plan

Use your current AHD floor plan for each story. Add detail lines in Revit or mark up a printed copy with a ruler and colored pens. Keep room names and fixtures readable under the pipe routes.

  1. Mark fixtures and plumbing walls

    Label every fixture and appliance that needs supply water, a drain, or both. Place the water heater. Mark walls that carry pipes and revise wall thickness where your design needs more room for piping. If fixtures share a wall, check whether they can use nearby piping.

  2. Run hot and cold supply

    Start the cold trunk at the house entrance from A. Branch to cold-water fixtures and to the heater. Start the hot trunk at the heater outlet and branch to fixtures that use hot water. Mark the inlet for the toilet with the longest cold-water route.

  3. Route drains and vents

    Join the fixture drains into a building drain and show the sewer lateral to B. Add arrows for downhill flow. Indicate traps for applicable fixtures, label the toilet’s built-in trap, and mark a cleanout. Show the vent paths and at least one vent through the roof. If a stack crosses floors, use the same label on each floor.

  4. Label a conservation choice

    Mark one specific choice on your plan. You could place the heater close to the fixtures that use hot water, insulate a hot-water line, or specify a low-flow fixture. Add a short note explaining what your choice saves.

Estimate pressure at the farthest toilet

Find the toilet with the longest cold-water pipe route from the house entrance. Measure along the pipe, including vertical segments, and use the reference below. The pipe and loss values are shared classroom assumptions for this estimate.

Jump to the two worked examples

Use the same assumptions

  • Starting pressure at the house entrance is 60 psi.
  • Assume ¾-inch copper pipe and use a classroom friction rate of 4 psi per 100 equivalent ft during flow.
  • Use 0.433 psi per ft for elevation change. Measure from the entrance to the toilet inlet. Record a rise as positive and a drop as negative.

Pressure formulas

  1. Add the fitting allowances. Multiply each fitting count by its allowance from the table, then add the results. Fitting allowance (ft) = sum of (count × ft per fitting)
  2. Find the equivalent length. Equivalent length (ft) = measured pipe length + fitting allowance
  3. Find the friction loss. Friction loss (psi) = equivalent length × (4 psi ÷ 100 ft)
  4. Find the signed elevation adjustment. Subtract the entrance elevation from the toilet inlet elevation to get the signed change in feet. Elevation adjustment (psi) = signed elevation change × 0.433 psi/ft
  5. Estimate the inlet pressure. Round the final result to one decimal place. Inlet pressure (psi) = 60 psi − friction loss − elevation adjustment

Worked examples

Follow the five steps, then repeat them with the measurements from your group’s plan.

Example 1 · Toilet 10 ft above the entrance

The route measures 92 ft, including vertical pipe. It passes through four 90° elbows, one straight-through tee, and one tee where the water turns into the branch.

  1. Fitting allowance(4 × 2 ft) + (1 × 1 ft) + (1 × 4 ft) = 13 ft
  2. Equivalent length92 ft + 13 ft = 105 ft
  3. Friction loss105 ft × (4 psi ÷ 100 ft) = 4.20 psi
  4. Elevation adjustment(+10 ft) × 0.433 psi/ft = +4.33 psi
  5. Inlet pressure60 psi − 4.20 psi − (+4.33 psi) = 51.47 psi

51.5 psi. Friction and the rise both reduce pressure at the toilet.

Example 2 · Toilet 4 ft below the entrance

The route measures 65 ft, including vertical pipe. It passes through two 90° elbows and one fully open shutoff valve.

  1. Fitting allowance(2 × 2 ft) + (1 × 1 ft) = 5 ft
  2. Equivalent length65 ft + 5 ft = 70 ft
  3. Friction loss70 ft × (4 psi ÷ 100 ft) = 2.80 psi
  4. Elevation adjustment(−4 ft) × 0.433 psi/ft = −1.732 psi
  5. Inlet pressure60 psi − 2.80 psi − (−1.732 psi) = 58.932 psi

58.9 psi. Subtracting a negative adds back 1.732 psi. The drop offsets some of the friction loss.
